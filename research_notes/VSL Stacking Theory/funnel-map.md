# VSL Stacking Theory funnel map

## What the theory claims

### Takeaway

The clearest public source is Josh Hamilton's LinkedIn post and transcript. He defines VSL stacking as placing a separate video at each funnel friction point where a person would normally ask a question or raise an objection. Each video sells the next step, instead of trying to close the entire journey at once. The post is a practitioner claim, not a controlled study of the full method.

### Cited Findings

- Hamilton says most businesses use video at one point, while VSL stacking puts video at every friction point in the funnel. He says each stage creates a different question in the buyer's head, so each video should handle that stage's objection and sell the next step. — [Josh Hamilton, “VSL Stacking Boosts Booking Rate and Sales”](https://www.linkedin.com/videos/josh-hamiltonx_vsl-stacking-theres-a-specific-strategy-activity-7511529327398686720-ZSiZ)
- In the same post, Hamilton's field-sales example uses four placements: a website VSL to sell a free report and generate bookings; a short follow-up for leads who do not pick up; a booking-confirmation video showing what will happen at the visit and who will arrive; and an optional in-home video on an iPad to standardize the pitch. — [Josh Hamilton](https://www.linkedin.com/videos/josh-hamiltonx_vsl-stacking-theres-a-specific-strategy-activity-7511529327398686720-ZSiZ)
- Hamilton also describes a post-appointment series that restates the offer, answers pricing objections, and prepares the buyer for the proposal or close. After purchase, he recommends a video that reinforces the decision and prepares onboarding or job start. — [Josh Hamilton](https://www.linkedin.com/videos/josh-hamiltonx_vsl-stacking-theres-a-specific-strategy-activity-7511529327398686720-ZSiZ)
- The public transcript names the intended business outcomes as separate numbers: booking rate, sit/show rate, and close rate. It says gains can compound, but the example's revenue projection is a target model, not independent proof. — [Josh Hamilton](https://www.linkedin.com/videos/josh-hamiltonx_vsl-stacking-theres-a-specific-strategy-activity-7511529327398686720-ZSiZ)
- Hamilton repeats the claim that people retain 95% of a video message versus 10% of text. The originator of that statistic, Insivia, says it came from a small, directional survey of about 200 B2B buyers and is not a peer-reviewed neuroscience finding or a universal law. — [Josh Hamilton](https://www.linkedin.com/videos/josh-hamiltonx_vsl-stacking-theres-a-specific-strategy-activity-7511529327398686720-ZSiZ); [Insivia's source note](https://www.insivia.com/why-does-video-convert-better-than-text/)

### Inferences

- The theory's reusable unit is not “a VSL page.” It is “one video per important decision.” A video earns its place when the next step creates a predictable question, fear, or justification problem.
- The strongest version of the theory is a sequencing idea: the first video should make the next action easier; later videos should remove the next new obstacle. Repeating the same sales pitch at every step would not be stacking in the useful sense.
- The theory does not prove that more videos always increase revenue. It predicts that stage-specific videos can improve the conversion rate of the transition they precede.

### Gaps

- I found no independent controlled study that tests the full VSL-stacking sequence from ad click through onboarding.
- The supplied Instagram reels could not be fetched because Instagram throttled the public fetch. The LinkedIn source was readable and used as the primary theory source.
- No public source establishes universal video lengths, mandatory stages, or a universal “best” number of videos.

## Evidence, examples, and measurement sources

### Takeaway

Public funnel guides agree on a simpler baseline: traffic goes to a focused video page, then to a form, application, calendar, checkout, or post-purchase step. Platform documentation supports measuring both video behavior and the downstream event path. The evidence also warns against adding video by default: large landing-page analyses found neutral or negative average effects, while controlled examples show that the right video can help and the wrong video can hurt.

### Cited Findings

- A common VSL funnel structure is traffic → VSL page → order form → upsell or confirmation. The page is intentionally focused around one video and one primary action. — [Asset Academy, VSL funnel structure](https://assetacademy.io/funnels/funnel-types/vsl-funnel-explained/)
- A high-ticket appointment version is traffic → VSL → qualifying question → application → confirmation and calendar access. This is an agency description, so its reported application and show-rate ranges should be treated as promotional claims, not benchmarks. — [Inflo Partners, high-ticket VSL funnel](https://www.inflopartners.com/blog/vsl-funnel-high-ticket-coaching)
- A commonly documented appointment funnel uses an opt-in page, VSL page, application page, scheduling page, and call-confirmation page. — [Skyline Social, VSL funnel](https://www.skylinesocial.com/vsl-funnel/)
- A VSL funnel can be used for a product purchase or to get a lead to book a sales call. The same source describes the video as the sales presentation, with the surrounding pages carrying the handoff. — [Skyline Social](https://www.skylinesocial.com/vsl-funnel/)
- GA4 Funnel exploration is designed to show the steps users take, where they succeed or fail, and where abandonment occurs. It supports open or closed funnels, direct or indirect next steps, time limits, and event-based conditions. — [Google Analytics, Funnel exploration](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9327974?hl=en-40); [Google Analytics funnel reports](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13012015?hl=en)
- Google Analytics events can measure page loads, link clicks, purchases, and custom interactions, and events can be marked as key events. This supports tracking video milestones and the next action as separate events. — [Google Analytics, Set up events](https://developers.google.com/analytics/devguides/collection/ga4/events)
- YouTube retention reporting shows gradual declines, spikes, and dips; it also reports the percentage still watching after the first 30 seconds, average view duration, and watch time. Google recommends moving compelling material earlier when strong moments occur late. — [YouTube Help, Measure key moments for audience retention](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en-GB)
- Wistia's audience analytics show what viewers watched, skipped, or rewatched, and mark interactions with forms, CTAs, and annotation links. For identified viewers, Wistia can show a timeline of touchpoints across videos and webinars. — [Wistia, Media Audience Analytics](https://support.wistia.com/en/articles/15556740-media-audience-analytics)
- Vimeo defines conversion-oriented video measurement around play rate, retention, finish rate, CTA clicks, and downstream conversion. Its guidance recommends tracking CTA clicks and conversions with unique URLs and web analytics rather than relying on views alone. — [Vimeo, Essential metrics](https://vimeo.com/blog/post/essential-metrics-and-tools-to-optimize-videos)
- Unbounce's analysis of about 35,000 landing pages found that pages with video had roughly similar or slightly worse conversion performance than pages without video, across goals, industries, traffic sources, and devices. Unbounce recommends testing video against a no-video variant and asking what job the video performs. — [Unbounce, Video on landing pages](https://unbounce.com/landing-pages/video-on-landing-pages-means-more-conversions-right-wrong-heres-why/)
- A randomized clinical-recruitment landing-page experiment tested three distinct videos against an original page. One video version had a 14.8% goal-conversion rate versus 13.3% for the original, while another had 2.7%; the study says the differences were not significant for the better video but the weakest video was significantly worse. Its sample was small and the trial ended early, so it is directional evidence that video quality and fit matter. — [A/B design testing of a clinical trial recruitment website, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8995844/)
- Vidyard's historical landing-page test reported 6.5% conversion without video, 11% with embedded video, and 13% with a lightbox video. This is a company case study, not independent research, and should be treated as an example rather than a benchmark. — [Unbounce case study citing Vidyard's test](https://unbounce.com/conversion-rate-optimization/case-study-using-video-to-lift-landing-page-conversion-rate-by-100/)

### Inferences

- The correct measurement unit is the transition after the video, not video engagement alone. A video can raise watch time while lowering leads, booked calls, or purchases if it delays or distracts from the action.
- The cleanest proof design is an A/B test with the same traffic, offer, page, and next step, changing only the video treatment. Then compare the intended downstream event and revenue-quality outcomes, not only play rate.
- Each stacked video should have a named event and a stage-specific control group where possible. Example event names: `micro_vsl_play`, `micro_vsl_50`, `micro_vsl_cta_click`, `lead_submit`, `calendar_booked`, `showed`, `checkout_started`, `purchase`, `onboarding_started`.

### Gaps

- Platform metrics vary by host. A player may report a play, an impression, an engaged view, or a completed view differently. Normalize definitions before comparing videos.
- Public sources rarely disclose enough traffic, audience quality, randomization, or revenue data to support a universal conversion benchmark.
- “Video helped” cannot be inferred from a high completion rate. Completion may reflect a small self-selected audience, while the video still fails to create the intended next action.

## Recommended operating model and end-to-end map

### Takeaway

Use micro-VSLs as targeted handoffs. At each stage, name the friction, answer one main objection, make one promise about the next step, and give one obvious action. Keep the video narrow enough that it does not reopen solved questions or replace the page's primary CTA. The table below is a recommended operating model derived from the theory plus the evidence; it is not presented as a direct claim made by Hamilton.

### Cited Findings

The operating model follows Hamilton's “one video per friction point” claim, the documented VSL page sequences from Skyline Social, Asset Academy, and Inflo Partners, and the measurement guidance from Google Analytics, YouTube, Wistia, Vimeo, and Unbounce. Those sources support the structure and measurement logic, but the exact scripts, lengths, and event names below are recommendations.

### Recommended funnel map

| Stage / audience | Friction point and likely objection | Video job | Cover | Avoid | Handoff / next action | Primary measurement |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ad click → landing page, cold lead generation | “Is this for me?” “What do I get for giving my details?” | Match the ad promise and earn the first low-risk action | Problem, specific audience, desired outcome, what the free asset or assessment gives, one proof point | Full offer pitch, every feature, long origin story, claims not supported by the ad | Form submit, quiz start, or free report request | Landing-page lead rate, cost per lead, video start rate, first-30-second retention, form start-to-submit |
| Lead captured → not yet booked | “Why should I talk now?” “Will this be a sales call?” | Turn the lead into a qualified next action | What happens next, who they will speak with, what they will receive, answer the two most common objections | Repeating the whole landing-page VSL, pressure, vague “we will reach out” language | Book a call, reply, or complete a qualification step | Lead-to-book rate, reply rate, qualification completion, time to book |
| Lead captured → no pickup / no response | “I need more information first.” “I do not recognize this number.” | Recover attention and reduce contact friction | A short human explanation of why the team called, the useful outcome of responding, and a low-effort way to continue | Guilt, repeated calls, fake urgency, a long VSL before the person has re-engaged | Watch → reply, call back, or choose a time | Contact rate, reply rate, booked rate from no-pickup segment, unsubscribes |
| Booked call → pre-call | “Will this be worth my time?” “What will happen?” “Who is showing up?” | Increase show-up quality and reduce anxiety | Agenda, expected duration, preparation, people involved, what a good outcome looks like, proof relevant to the buyer | New claims, a second hard close, making the call feel mandatory or risky | Confirm attendance, complete prep, attend | Show rate, reschedule rate, cancellation rate, pre-call completion, close rate by watched/not-watched |
| Booked call → high-ticket qualification | “Am I a fit?” “Do I have the budget or authority?” | Pre-qualify and make the call useful for both sides | Fit criteria, who should not book, decision process, broad investment framing if appropriate | Hiding material requirements, promising a guaranteed result, forcing an unqualified call | Application, qualification form, or calendar confirmation | Qualified-book rate, no-show rate, disqualification rate, sales-cycle time |
| Discovery call → proposal / second appointment | “Can this solve my exact situation?” “Why this price?” | Carry the explanation forward after the live call | Recap of the problem, recommended path, proof or walkthrough tied to their context, pricing logic, implementation steps | Generic replay, invented personalization, making the video a substitute for the agreed proposal | Review proposal, attend second call, approve next step | Proposal-to-close rate, second-meeting attendance, time to decision, objection frequency |
| Ecommerce product page → checkout | “Will this work for me?” “How does it look or work?” “Is it worth the price?” | Demonstrate the product and remove one purchase objection | Product in use, fit or setup, key benefit, proof, guarantee/returns stated accurately | Hiding price, autoplay with sound, delaying add-to-cart, a brand film that does not answer a buying question | Add to cart or buy now, visible beside or immediately after video | Add-to-cart rate, checkout start, purchase conversion, revenue per visitor, video-assisted purchase rate |
| Ecommerce cart / checkout abandonment | “Do I trust this?” “Can I wait?” “Did I choose correctly?” | Recover the decision without adding new confusion | Product reminder, shipping/returns, sizing or setup answer, concise reason to finish now if real | Fake scarcity, changing the offer without explanation, forcing a video before checkout can resume | Return to checkout or ask support | Recovery rate, recovered revenue, refund rate, support contacts, time to purchase |
| Purchase / order confirmation | “Did I make the right choice?” “What happens now?” | Reinforce the decision and prevent buyer's remorse | Thank-you, order status, first success step, what to expect, support path | Upselling before confirming the purchase, changing terms, overloading with training | Confirm account, download, schedule kickoff, or complete first setup | Activation, support tickets, refund/cancellation rate, time to first value |
| Onboarding / first success | “Where do I start?” “Can I do this?” | Get the customer to the first meaningful result | One next step, short walkthrough, success definition, common setup mistake, where to get help | Full course overview, five CTAs, advanced features before the first win | Complete setup, submit intake, use product, or attend kickoff | Onboarding completion, time to first value, activation, early churn, usage of the intended feature |
| Customer result / referral | “Was this worth it?” “Can I trust you enough to refer?” | Turn a result into retention, proof, or referral | What success looks like, how to capture a result, referral or testimonial request with a clear choice | Asking for a testimonial before value, coaching the customer to invent claims | Submit result, book review, refer, or renew | Retention, referral rate, testimonial completion, expansion revenue |

### Offer-type changes

- Lead generation: the first video should sell the information exchange or assessment, not the entire paid offer. The next step is usually a form, quiz, or report request. Optimize for qualified lead rate and downstream booked or sales-qualified rate, not cheap leads alone.
- Booked calls: the most valuable stack is usually after the lead submits: no-pickup recovery, pre-call expectation setting, qualification, and post-call proposal support. The handoff should make attendance and decision quality easier. Measure show rate, qualified show rate, close rate, and revenue per lead.
- Ecommerce: video should demonstrate, compare, fit, install, or prove. It should not hide the buy button or make a simple purchase feel like a sales call. Measure add-to-cart, checkout, purchase, revenue per visitor, refunds, and assisted conversions. Test video against a clean product page because large page analyses show video can be neutral or negative.
- High-ticket: video can transfer education and risk reduction out of the sales call, but it should not pretend to replace trust, fit checks, or a custom proposal. Use contextual proof, clear fit criteria, decision-process guidance, and a direct handoff to application, calendar, proposal, or second appointment. Measure qualified bookings, show rate, close rate, sales-cycle time, and cash collected.

### What every micro-VSL should avoid

- Do not make the video solve every stage at once.
- Do not introduce a new promise that the next page cannot fulfill.
- Do not use completion rate as the sole success metric.
- Do not hide the next action until an arbitrary timestamp if the viewer is ready sooner; test the timing.
- Do not add a video where the page already answers the question better in one sentence or where the video delays a simple CTA.
- Do not use unsupported proof, invented personalization, fake scarcity, or an objection that the audience did not actually raise.

### Measurement plan

1. Give every video a stage ID, audience segment, version, and CTA destination.
2. Track `impression/load`, `play`, `25%/50%/75%` or equivalent milestones, `CTA click`, and the stage's intended conversion event.
3. Pass the video ID and version into the next-step event, CRM record, and purchase or booked-call record.
4. Report the local metric and the downstream metric together. Example: “VSL-03 50% watch rate; booked-call rate among exposed visitors; qualified show rate; revenue per lead.”
5. Use holdouts or A/B tests where volume allows. Compare exposed versus control on the intended business outcome, not just watch time.
6. Review the retention graph against the objection timeline. A dip at the objection means the section may be unclear or mistimed; a strong view rate with weak CTA clicks means the video may educate without asking clearly.
7. For post-purchase videos, use activation, time to first value, refund, support, and retention. A video that lowers support tickets or refunds can be valuable even if it has no direct CTA click.

### Gaps

- There is no reliable public benchmark for “good” micro-VSL completion, booking lift, show-rate lift, close-rate lift, or revenue lift across all offers.
- The theory's field-sales revenue example does not publish experiment design, control data, sample size, or confidence intervals.
- The exact best placement depends on the current journey. The recommended map should be treated as a testable starting model, not a mandatory stack.
