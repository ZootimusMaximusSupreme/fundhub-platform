# VSL stacking turns funnel friction into videos

VSL Stacking Theory is the idea of placing a separate video sales letter at each important point where a buyer must make a new decision. **Each video sells the next step, not the whole journey.** A first video might sell a report request, a second might sell a booked call, and a third might sell attendance or purchase. The framework is most useful for complex, high-trust offers with repeated questions between stages. It is not a proven law, and no reliable public experiment has shown that a stack beats one strong VSL. The honest way to use it is as a testable operating hypothesis: find a real friction point, make one focused video for it, and keep the video only if it improves the downstream business result.

## The phrase is new; the underlying tactic is stage-specific selling

The clearest public source is Josh Hamilton’s October 2026 LinkedIn post. He says, “I call it VSL stacking,” and describes putting video “at every single point where a human would normally have to answer a question or handle an objection.” His example uses a website video, a no-contact follow-up video, a pre-appointment video, and an optional in-home iPad video. ([Josh Hamilton on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/josh-hamiltonx_vsl-stacking-theres-a-specific-strategy-activity-7511529327398686720-ZSiZ))

That source documents a practitioner’s name and model. It does **not** prove that Hamilton coined the phrase, that the method is an established industry standard, or that his projected lifts happened. No book, peer-reviewed paper, stable standard, or independently audited full-funnel case study was found. The supplied Instagram reels could not be independently inspected through the research tool, so their dates, authors, and claims remain unverified: [reel 1](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DadGnlYt9tt/), [reel 2](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DeHX8vivm3A/), and [reel 3](https://www.instagram.com/reel/Dd4CFzZPnFt/).

The idea extends a normal VSL. A conventional VSL is usually one linear video with one main action, such as buying, applying, or booking. ([Vidpros](https://vidpros.com/video-sales-letter-funnel/); [Rob Palmer](https://robpalmer.com/blog/what-is-a-vsl)) VSL stacking distributes that persuasion across the buyer’s journey. It is also different from “stacking” proof, bonuses, or value inside one long VSL. In plain English: **one long VSL is one sales conversation; a stack is a set of short sales conversations timed to different decisions.**

## The funnel works by selling one next decision at a time

The end-to-end model below is a recommended operating model derived from Hamilton’s description, normal VSL funnel structures, and measurement guidance. It is not a proven universal sequence.

| Funnel point | Buyer’s likely question | What the micro-VSL should sell | Handoff | Main metric |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ad click → landing page | “Is this for me, and what do I get?” | The report, assessment, quiz, application, or purchase | Submit, start, or buy | Qualified lead or purchase rate |
| Lead captured → no booking | “Why should I talk now?” | The reply, booking, or qualification step | Book, reply, or finish qualification | Lead-to-book rate and qualified rate |
| No pickup / no response | “Who called, and why answer?” | A low-effort re-entry into the conversation | Reply, call back, or choose a time | Contact and booked rate |
| Call booked → appointment | “What will happen, and is it worth my time?” | A confident, informed show-up | Confirm, prepare, and attend | Show rate, reschedule rate, qualified show rate |
| High-ticket qualification | “Am I a fit, and what is expected?” | An honest fit check and useful application | Apply or confirm calendar | Qualified-book rate and disqualification rate |
| Discovery → proposal | “Will this solve my situation, and why this price?” | Proposal review or second meeting | Review, attend, approve, or buy | Proposal-to-close rate and time to decision |
| Product page → checkout | “How does it work, fit, or compare?” | The purchase decision | Add to cart or buy | Purchase rate and revenue per visitor |
| Checkout abandonment | “Can I trust this, and did I choose right?” | Return to checkout | Resume checkout | Recovered revenue and refunds |
| Purchase → onboarding | “What happens now?” | The first successful use, not another sale | Set up, schedule, or start | Activation and time to first value |
| Result → referral / renewal | “Was it worth it?” | A review, referral, renewal, or next useful action | Refer, renew, or submit proof | Retention, referral, and expansion revenue |

The core unit is not “one video per page.” It is **one video per unresolved decision**. A video earns its place when the next step creates a predictable question, fear, or justification problem. The next video should answer the new question created by the next stage. Replaying the same hook, origin story, proof, and CTA at every stage creates video clutter, not a useful stack.

### What each video must do

The first video should match the ad promise, explain the problem and immediate benefit, provide one credible proof point, and make the first action feel safe. It should sell the information exchange or assessment rather than forcing a cold visitor through the entire paid-offer pitch.

The second video should handle the reason a captured lead has not booked, replied, or answered. It should explain what happens next, who the prospect will speak with, what they will receive, and why the step is worth the time. It should not repeat the first VSL word for word.

The pre-call video should sell attendance and preparation. It can show the agenda, expected duration, people involved, useful preparation, and what a good outcome looks like. Hamilton’s public example describes this as a way to explain the visit and improve the appointment sit rate. ([Josh Hamilton](https://www.linkedin.com/videos/josh-hamiltonx_vsl-stacking-theres-a-specific-strategy-activity-7511529327398686720-ZSiZ))

The proposal or close-support video should sell the next decision with context: the recommended path, the reason for the price, relevant proof, implementation steps, and the agreed handoff. It should not invent personalization or pretend to replace a custom proposal or live judgment.

The post-purchase video should sell the first successful use. It should confirm the decision, explain what happens next, give one starting action, and show where help lives. It is better described as onboarding or activation video than as a classic VSL.

### Example sequence for a home-services offer

Imagine a homeowner clicks an ad offering a free roof or home assessment. **Video 1, “See what your assessment includes,”** sells the form and sets the value of the report. After the form is submitted, **Video 2, “Why we are calling,”** explains who will respond, what the homeowner will receive, and how to book. Once the visit is booked, **Video 3, “What happens during the visit,”** sells the show-up by explaining the agenda, timing, and people involved. After the visit, **Video 4, “Your recommended next step,”** supports proposal review with the relevant proof, price logic, and implementation path. After signature, **Video 5, “What happens now,”** sells a smooth start and the first onboarding action. This sequence follows Hamilton’s public example, but the sequence and titles are an operating example, not a proven template. ([Josh Hamilton](https://www.linkedin.com/videos/josh-hamiltonx_vsl-stacking-theres-a-specific-strategy-activity-7511529327398686720-ZSiZ))

## Offer type changes the best stack

For lead generation, the stack should be light. The first video sells the form, quiz, report, or assessment. Later video can sell the booking or qualification step. The main danger is collecting cheap leads that never become qualified opportunities, so measure downstream booked or sales-qualified rate rather than lead cost alone.

For booked-call and high-ticket offers, the strongest use case is usually after the lead submits. No-pickup recovery, pre-call expectation setting, fit checks, and proposal support can move repeated education out of the sales call. The videos can make calls more focused, but they do not prove that asynchronous video replaces trust, diagnosis, negotiation, or a human close.

For ecommerce, video should demonstrate, compare, fit, install, or prove. It should not hide the buy button or make a simple purchase feel like a sales appointment. A large Baymard product-page study found that 41% of tested users watched product videos and 59% did not; discoverability and placement mattered. ([Baymard Institute](https://baymard.com/research-articles/embedding-product-page-videos)) Test the video against a clean product page and watch purchase rate, revenue per visitor, refunds, and support contacts.

For onboarding and retention, the video’s job is activation, time to first value, support reduction, or renewal. Do not judge it by a sales CTA. A video that lowers refunds or support demand can be valuable even if it produces no click.

## Evidence supports testing video, not the full theory

The evidence is uneven. It supports the mechanics of measuring video and the idea that video can help in some settings. It does not support a general claim that more videos create compounding revenue.

| Statement | Evidence class | What the evidence actually shows |
|---|---|---|
| VSL stacking means a video at each friction point | Practitioner claim / direct documentation | Hamilton publicly defines and illustrates the term. This documents the framework, not its causal effect. ([LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/josh-hamiltonx_vsl-stacking-theres-a-specific-strategy-activity-7511529327398686720-ZSiZ)) |
| The Hamilton example produces 10–20% more bookings, 20% more shows, and 5–10% more closes | Practitioner target / unsupported result claim | The post gives percentages and a revenue projection but no control, sample, dates, observed results, or independent verification. Treat as a forecast, not a benchmark. |
| Video can improve persuasion or belief in some contexts | Stronger adjacent evidence | A large preregistered political-communication program found only a modest persuasion advantage for video over text. That is not a funnel test, but it argues against “video always wins.” ([PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8617416/)) |
| The right video can help a landing-page action | Controlled but indirect evidence | A VWO case reported sign-ups rising from 11.9% to 15.3% after changing CTA framing to “Watch the Video.” It tests CTA framing, not a VSL stack, and omits key design details. ([VWO](https://wingify.com/success-stories/the-social-man/)) |
| The wrong video can hurt conversion | Stronger adjacent evidence | A randomized clinical-recruitment landing-page study reported one video at 14.8%, the original at 13.3%, and another video at 2.7%; the weak version was significantly worse. The study was small and ended early. ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8995844/)) |
| Video is not automatically a conversion lift | Large observational evidence | Unbounce’s analysis of about 35,000 landing pages found video pages roughly similar or slightly worse overall than pages without video. It recommends testing video only when it has a clear job. ([Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/landing-pages/video-on-landing-pages-means-more-conversions-right-wrong-heres-why/)) |
| Removing video can improve action rate | Large randomized adjacent evidence | NextAfter reported 82,675 visitors in a donation test where a no-video treatment converted at 0.24% versus 0.08% for the video control. The treatment also changed the written story, so this is a warning about friction, not a pure video-only test. ([NextAfter](https://www.nextafter.com/experiments/how-video-donation-page-affects-donor-conversion/)) |
| Video behavior and downstream events can be measured | Platform documentation | GA4, Wistia, Vimeo, and YouTube document play, retention, CTA, and conversion measurement. These tools support a test; they do not prove the strategy. ([GA4 events](https://developers.google.com/analytics/devguides/collection/ga4/events), [Wistia analytics](https://support.wistia.com/en/articles/15556740-media-audience-analytics), [Vimeo metrics](https://vimeo.com/blog/post/essential-metrics-and-tools-to-optimize-videos)) |
| A stack beats one long VSL | No direct evidence found | No public controlled experiment was found that holds offer, traffic, message, sales team, and follow-up constant while comparing one VSL with multiple micro-VSLs. |

The popular “95% retention for video versus 10% for text” claim should not be used as proof. The cited source describes a small, directional survey of about 200 B2B buyers, not a peer-reviewed universal law. ([Insivia source note](https://www.insivia.com/why-does-video-convert-better-than-text/))

## Compare one VSL with a stack by the job, not by novelty

| Format | Strength | Weakness | Best fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| One long VSL | One coherent story and one clear CTA; simpler production and tracking | Must answer every important question before the first action; can become long or generic | Simple offers, one main decision, audiences that already understand the category |
| VSL stack | Matches explanation to intent; can reduce repeated human answers; lets each transition have its own KPI | More production, page weight, tracking, fatigue, and cross-stage attribution risk | Complex offers, high trust, multiple objections, calls, applications, or long sales cycles |
| Webinar / replay | Can teach a broad idea and create one large sales moment | High time cost and event-like commitment | Education-heavy offers where the audience will give sustained attention |
| Sales call | Can diagnose, adapt, negotiate, and build trust | Costly, variable, and hard to scale | High-stakes or highly individualized decisions |

A single VSL is usually the better starting point when the offer is easy to understand, the CTA is low friction, the buyer has one main objection, or the real problem is weak relevance and message-market fit. A stack becomes more plausible when the buyer must make several different decisions and each decision has a known drop-off or repeated question.

## Stacks fail when they add work instead of certainty

The first failure mode is repetition: every video repeats the same promise, proof, origin story, and CTA. The second is misplaced timing: a long video appears before a simple form or buy button and delays a ready buyer. The third is production convenience: the team orders videos by what is easy to film instead of by the buyer’s next unanswered question. The fourth is metric confusion: the team celebrates watch time or completion while qualified leads, shows, purchases, or profit fall. The fifth is measurement failure: viewers self-select into later videos, events duplicate, cross-device paths break, or the CRM cannot connect exposure to revenue.

Other common failures are technical and operational. Audible autoplay can be blocked or feel intrusive. Extra players can slow the page. Captions, text alternatives, and visible terms can be missed. A video can create curiosity without fit, raise CTA clicks while lowering qualified conversion, or produce a false attribution win because motivated people watched more. The public evidence supports these as risks: Unbounce found video can be neutral or negative, browsers restrict autoplay, and Google warns that poorly designed events can create noisy engagement data. ([Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/landing-pages/video-on-landing-pages-means-more-conversions-right-wrong-heres-why/); [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Media/Guides/Autoplay); [GA4 sessions](https://developers.google.com/analytics/devguides/collection/ga4/sessions))

## Production and tracking must be designed before filming

The lowest-risk production model is one modular shoot that creates three focused cuts: problem and mechanism, proof and objection, and CTA and next step. This avoids filming a full library before the first test. Each asset needs a stable stage ID, audience segment, version, CTA destination, transcript, captions, thumbnail or poster, owner, and source file. The viewer must still understand the offer and act if autoplay fails, sound is off, the player does not load, or the user skips the video. Browsers commonly block audible autoplay, so do not make the funnel depend on it. ([MDN autoplay guide](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Media/Guides/Autoplay))

Track the video and the intended business event together. At minimum, record experiment assignment, video ID and version, page load, play, qualified play, 25/50/75% progress, pause, seek, replay, CTA click, and the next-step event. Carry the video ID and version into the CRM record, booked-call record, order, refund, cancellation, and revenue record. GA4 supports custom events and funnel exploration; Google also warns that artificial engagement events can create noise. ([GA4 events](https://developers.google.com/analytics/devguides/collection/ga4/events); [GA4 funnel exploration](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9327974?hl=en-40))

Judge each video on the transition it is meant to improve. A pre-call video is successful when qualified show rate or revenue per lead improves, not when completion rate is high. A product video is successful when purchase economics improve, not when viewers watch to the end. Watch depth is diagnostic evidence. It is not causal evidence, because people who keep watching were already more motivated.

Plan for speed, accessibility, privacy, and policy. Extra players and events add page weight and measurement failure points. Google’s Core Web Vitals guidance targets LCP below 2.5 seconds, INP below 200 milliseconds, and CLS below 0.1. ([Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals)) Use captions, a visible text CTA, a poster image, and no required autoplay. Make required terms and material business information visible on the page instead of hiding them in a later video. Analytics consent and financial-ad restrictions can also affect audience and event design. ([ICO cookies guidance](https://ico.org.uk/media/1545/cookies_guidance.pdf); [Google financial products policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/2464998?hl=en))

## Test the smallest useful stack first

The practical minimum viable test is **one extra 60–90 second micro-VSL**. Put it after one known objection and before the next action. Use the current one-VSL or no-video path as the control. Randomize visitors before exposure. Keep the offer, price, page copy, proof, form, traffic, follow-up, sales team, and page-speed budget constant. Do not add new retargeting logic in the first test.

Pre-register one primary metric: qualified lead rate, booked-call rate, qualified show rate, purchase rate, revenue per visitor, or contribution margin. Add guardrails for load time, bounce, form completion, qualification, no-show, refund, cancellation, and support contacts. Check assignment integrity, duplicate assignment, event delivery, and cross-device or cross-domain leakage. Run long enough to capture the real downstream outcome. Analyze only a few pre-specified segments, such as device, source, and new versus returning visitor.

Keep the video if the primary downstream outcome improves enough to cover production and maintenance cost, the gain survives basic quality checks, and the video answers a distinct question. Change it if watch depth is good but CTA clicks are weak, if clicks rise but qualified conversion falls, if mobile performance drops, or if only warm visitors benefit. Remove it if the primary outcome falls, the video delays a simple CTA, it repeats the main VSL without adding belief or clarity, or the measured value does not cover its operating cost.

## Decide with this framework

Start with the buyer’s journey, not a desire to use more video. If there is no known friction, no repeated question, and no measurable next step, do not add a VSL. If the offer has one simple decision, test or improve one VSL first. If the offer has several high-friction decisions, map each transition and rank the friction points by lost value. Build only the first micro-VSL for the highest-value unresolved question.

Then ask five questions. Can the video sell one clear next action? Can the team measure that action and the downstream business result? Can the page work without autoplay, sound, or a third-party callback? Can production and maintenance fit the expected gain? Can the test separate the video’s effect from audience selection and sales-team changes? If the answer to any of these is no, fix the measurement or the funnel before building a stack.

The decision rule is simple: **use VSL stacking when it removes a measured obstacle better than a short block of copy, a proof element, or a human handoff; use one VSL when one coherent explanation is enough; use no video when video adds time without adding clarity.** The public evidence supports this testing discipline. It does not support promising compounding lifts, treating Hamilton’s targets as benchmarks, or calling VSL stacking proven.

## Conclusion

VSL stacking is best understood as a modular sales system, not a magic number of videos. Its strongest idea is that the buyer’s questions change as the journey advances, so the explanation should change too. Its weakest idea is the leap from plausible stage-specific persuasion to guaranteed compounding revenue. The marketer who treats every micro-VSL as an accountable handoff—with one job, one next action, one control, and one downstream metric—can learn whether the method fits the offer without committing to an expensive video library.
