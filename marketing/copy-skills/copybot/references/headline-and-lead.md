# Headline and Lead

## Headline

Attract the right audience and give that reader a reason to continue. A strong headline may use self-interest, news, relevant curiosity, or a quick/easy framing. It need not use all four. Never use cleverness, shock, or curiosity merely to attract unrelated eyeballs.

### 22 optional starters (adapted from Cashvertising)

Use these as adaptable structures, not copy-and-paste headlines:

1. FREE: [offer or benefit]
2. NEW: [new product, discovery, method, event, or benefit]
3. AT LAST: [something the audience has been waiting for]
4. THIS: [specific thing and what it does]
5. ANNOUNCING: [new product, development, release, or offer]
6. WARNING: [genuine problem, danger, or important information]
7. JUST RELEASED: [new information, product, study, discovery, or development]
8. NOW: [something the reader can now truthfully do or obtain]
9. HERE'S: [how someone did something or what the reader will discover]
10. THESE: [number or group of things plus result or question]
11. WHICH OF: [choices or alternatives relevant to the reader]
12. FINALLY: [long-awaited solution, product, benefit, or development]
13. LOOK: [attention-worthy announcement or opportunity]
14. PRESENTING: [product, method, solution, or opportunity]
15. INTRODUCING: [new product, service, or solution]
16. HOW: [how to achieve a desired result]
17. AMAZING: [supported surprising fact, product, result, or development]
18. DO YOU: [question about a relevant problem, desire, ability, or situation]
19. WOULD YOU: [question involving an offer, trade, result, or opportunity]
20. CAN YOU: [question involving something important to the reader]
21. IF YOU: [identify the reader, problem, preference, or situation and connect the message]
22. STARTING TODAY: [supported result or improvement the reader can begin now]

Choose a starter because it fits truthful information. Do not invent news, urgency, ease, danger, novelty, proof, or results to satisfy a structure.

## Lead

The lead is the opening body copy immediately after the headline. Continue the state of mind the headline created; do not restart the advertisement.

### Choose the approach before the opening device

Use the reader context available. A familiar product and trusted promise may support a direct opening; an unfamiliar idea or skeptical reader may need recognition, explanation, or a relevant story first. Awareness is a guide, not a deterministic lookup table: an experienced but jaded reader may resist another direct promise.

Six lead families from Great Leads:

| Family | Useful when | Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Existing offer | Readers already understand the value and care about the supplied terms | Present actual terms; do not create an offer |
| Promise | A desired benefit is recognizable and believable | Match the strength of the promise to support; smaller can be more credible |
| Problem–solution | Readers recognize a problem more readily than the solution | Demonstrate understanding without inventing or exaggerating their pain |
| Secret | A relevant information gap creates useful curiosity | Give concrete relevance and an honest payoff; avoid empty or misleading concealment |
| Proclamation | A consequential statement or development deserves attention | Support the assertion and connect it to the reader |
| Story | An event makes the central idea recognizable or emotionally meaningful | Use a supplied/sourced story or clearly hypothetical illustration; earn the transition |

Hybrids are allowed. Choose a coherent direction, not a rigid template. An indirect opening must earn its space and reach the point before curiosity becomes frustration.

### Rule of One

Organize around one central idea and intended response. Supporting points and shifts of emotion are allowed when they develop that direction. Do not require a story, a product, or a sale in every lead.

### 12 optional opening devices (adapted from Cashvertising)

Choose the one that best continues the headline and supports the Rule of One:

1. Continue the thought introduced by the headline.
2. Ask a question that naturally follows the headline.
3. Quote a relevant respected authority whose statement is supplied or sourced.
4. Give the reader a free taste of the method, information, or value.
5. Challenge the reader to test or prove the claim when it can reasonably be verified.
6. Start with genuine skepticism, then show what changed it.
7. Tell what others are saying using supplied, attributable reactions or testimonials.
8. Play reporter for genuine news, research, findings, releases, developments, or events.
9. Get personal with "you" and the reader's situation, frustration, problem, or desire.
10. Tell one dramatic story that advances the same central idea and emotion.
11. Give super-detailed, supported specifications when the details add appeal.
12. Lure the reader with a very short first sentence that fits the subject and emotion.

These are options that may be combined when coherent, not 12 requirements or mutually exclusive labels. Never invent authority, research, testimonials, tests, specifications, stories, or news to use an approach.

A provisional opening can guide drafting; finalize it against what the body actually delivers. If the body offers no useful insight or payoff to open with, improve its substance rather than adding empty suspense. Reader awareness, the asset's purpose, and presentation format inform selection without being interchangeable labels or a compulsory stage sequence.

Use only the opening length the assignment needs. A social hook may be one line; a long sales letter may warrant a developed lead. Do not pad to a word quota or force a short opening at the expense of necessary meaning.
