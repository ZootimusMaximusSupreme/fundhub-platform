# Argument and components

## Develop belief through sequence

For substantial copy, distinguish:
- Central promise or point: what the piece ultimately asks the reader to accept.
- Supporting claims: the statements needed to make that point meaningful.
- Explanation: how something works or why a conclusion follows.
- Evidence: applicable observations, demonstrations, records, or other support actually available.

An analogy can explain a claim. It does not establish that the claim is true. A plausible mechanism is not demonstrated efficacy. A testimonial supports what that person reports, not a universal result.

Build from a recognizable starting point toward the claim. If a passage requires belief the preceding copy has not earned, add the missing supported explanation, move the passage, narrow the claim, or remove it. Do not compensate with hype.

Place support close enough to the claim to answer skepticism when it arises. Do not mechanically defer all evidence to a "proof section." Vary between promise, explanation, example, evidence, and implication according to the reader's next need. Blend them where natural.

Review the consequential claims internally: what supports each, how far does that support reach, and is its placement useful? If support is missing, write a narrower supported statement, omit it, or flag the exact gap. Do not interrupt every ordinary sentence with an evidence demand.

## Develop paragraphs

Introduce a core idea, elaborate it as needed, then bridge to the next. Useful elaborations include example, explanation, reasons, cause and effect, comparison, contrast, definition, narrative sequence, instructions, or evidence. Choose what resolves the reader's question; do not cycle through a catalog.

Transitions should express real relationships. Adding "and here's the thing" cannot fix a logical jump. Natural curiosity comes from a meaningful unanswered question that the copy actually resolves.

## Choose the useful development

These are questions for judgment, not mandatory sections:

- What is unclear about how an event or process works? Explain the relevant sequence or demonstrate it.
- Why should the conclusion follow? Give the reason and check that the connection holds.
- What establishes the factual claim? Place applicable evidence near it, or narrow the claim if support is absent.
- What remains abstract? Use a concrete action, example, or observation from the available material.
- What fresh perspective adds meaning? Develop the same idea through a different consequence, comparison, or viewpoint; remove repetition that adds nothing.
- What expectation needs resolution? Deliver the information the opening or preceding passage has made relevant.

A passage can do several of these at once. One technique can also operate across a whole piece.

## Benefits and specificity

Connect a supplied feature to a defensible practical consequence. Do not extend that consequence into unsupported outcomes.

## Objections

Address relevant doubts from the supplied material or clear features of the situation. Distinguish a plausible reader question from a documented customer objection. Answer with available facts, explanation, support, or existing terms; never invent a guarantee to remove resistance.

## Existing offer and CTA

When the assignment includes an offer, clarify what is provided and its supplied conditions. Do not design bundles, bonuses, pricing, or guarantees. If a CTA is appropriate, make the action specific and explain what happens next only when known. A piece may end with an insight, implication, or question when that better fulfills the assignment.

## Opening

The opening creates an expectation the body must fulfill; neither curiosity nor a promise should lead to a bait-and-switch.
